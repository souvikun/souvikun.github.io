ENGINEERING FIELD NOTES / 001

Thinking in circuits.

There is something satisfying about reducing a complicated problem to a few blocks, connections and measurable signals.

06 MIN READ SEP 2026

Engineering often begins with a deceptively simple question: what is actually happening here?

A circuit diagram gives us a useful way to answer that question. Instead of looking at an entire system as one overwhelming object, we can split it into sources, components, paths and outputs.

A resistor limits current. A capacitor stores energy. An inductor resists changes in current. A transistor can become a switch or an amplifier. Suddenly a complicated machine becomes a collection of understandable pieces.

V = I × R

The interesting part is that these relationships are not merely mathematical abstractions. They describe physical behavior. The equation becomes useful when we can measure something, change something, and observe the result.

That is probably what I enjoy most about electrical engineering: the loop between theory and reality. You calculate something on paper, build it, measure it, discover that your assumptions were slightly wrong, and then try again.

Build. Measure. Debug. Repeat.

SIGNALS FIELD NOTES / 002

Signal, noise and everything between.

Information becomes interesting when something interferes with it. A small meditation on useful patterns and noise.

04 MIN READ SEP 2026

A signal is useful because it carries information. But every real system has something else riding along with it.

Thermal effects, electromagnetic interference, imperfect components and limitations in measurement all introduce noise.

SNR = P(signal) / P(noise)

The challenge is not necessarily to eliminate every unwanted component. Sometimes that is impossible. The more useful question is: can we distinguish what matters?

Filters are one answer. Averaging is another. Better sensors help. Better shielding helps. But there is also a conceptual lesson here: useful information is often surrounded by irrelevant information.

Learning to separate the two is useful far beyond electrical engineering.

PERSONAL FIELD NOTES / 003

Why build anything?

Because sometimes the best way to understand something is to take it apart and make your own version from scratch.

03 MIN READ SEP 2026

There is a strange satisfaction in creating a working version of something that already exists.

You could simply use the finished tool, but building your own version forces you to understand the decisions hiding underneath the interface.

You start asking different questions. Why does this component exist? Why is this value chosen? What happens when I change it?

Not everything needs to become a serious project. Sometimes the value is simply learning something that you could not have understood by reading about it.

Make the tiny version. Break it. Fix it. Then make it stranger.
